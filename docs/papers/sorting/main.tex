\title{Name Sort: Bucketing Person Names by Character Frequency,\\
and When MSD Radix Sort Is Enough}
\author{Attila Torda}

\begin{abstract}
Person names are far from uniformly distributed: in the 2010 US Census,
\so{share@M}\,\% of people have a surname beginning with M and
\so{share@X}\,\% one beginning with X, and single prefixes such as
\emph{Ma} or \emph{Wil} hold more than one percent each. We ask whether a
sort can profit from knowing this in advance. \emph{Name sort} is a bucket
sort whose buckets are cut from character-frequency tables, one per
position for the first three characters of a name, built from public Census
data and refined on a small sample of the input. It cuts a trie of at most
three levels so that each leaf is expected to hold about 32 names, packs the
leaves into buckets, distributes the names in two linear passes and finishes
each bucket with insertion sort or MSD radix sort. On a million synthetic
``Surname Firstname'' names it is \so{syn@gcc@1000000@vsstd}$\times$ faster
than \texttt{std::sort} and \so{syn@gcc@1000000@vsmsd}$\times$ faster than a
plain MSD radix sort (GCC; MSVC: \so{syn@msvc@1000000@vsstd}$\times$ and
\so{syn@msvc@1000000@vsmsd}$\times$). The evaluation is mostly a
negative-leaning one, though. On English names the frequency tables add
nothing measurable over the sample alone; deeper tries that cut more even
buckets are slower; the plan pays only from about \so{abl@cross@names-1m}
synthetic names; and on the \so{data@surnames} real Census surnames plain MSD
radix sort is faster. We
report this together with sorts that the standard libraries of C, C++, Java,
Python, Rust, Go, C\# and JavaScript do not offer: an LSD radix sort (up to
\so{num@gcc@u32@uniform@16777216@radix@x}$\times$ faster than
\texttt{std::sort} on 16\,Mi keys), a sampled bucket sort, an AVX2 bitonic
sort for any $n$, a bitonic merge sort with an AVX2 merge network
(\so{h031@u32@uniform@16777216@bitonic_merge@x}$\times$), and a GPU bitonic
sort through OpenCL (\so{h031@u32@uniform@16777216@bitonic_gpu@x}$\times$,
copies included), all in an open-source C library with every number in this
paper regenerated from its benchmark files.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Sorting names is among the most common jobs of everyday software: contact
lists, member and patient registers, class lists, mailing lists. Library
sorts treat names as opaque strings and compare them,
$\Theta(n \log n)$ times, each comparison reading bytes that the previous
ones have mostly read already. String sorts that inspect characters
instead---MSD radix sort~\cite{mcilroy1993radix,karkkainen2008radix},
multikey quicksort~\cite{bentley1997strings},
burstsort~\cite{sinha2004burstsort}---avoid most of this, but they learn the
distribution of the characters as they go.

Names, however, have a distribution that is known before the input arrives.
Public statistics list how many people carry each surname and first
name~\cite{census2010,census1990}, and the distribution is very uneven
(Section~\ref{sec:data}). A sort that knows where the dense regions are can
cut buckets of equal expected size in one step, the idea behind
samplesort~\cite{frazer1970samplesort,sanders2004superscalar,axtmann2017ips4o}
and learned sorting~\cite{kristo2020learned}, but with the model fitted on
millions of real names instead of the input.

This paper reports what that buys. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Name sort} (Section~\ref{sec:namesort}): a bucket sort whose
  bucket boundaries come from per-position character-frequency tables of
  person names, blended with a sample of the input. A small trie of lookup
  tables distributes the names, and every bucket is finished with a
  comparison sort or MSD radix sort that skips the bytes its prefix fixes.
  The order is byte order (UTF-8 code point order) or ASCII-caseless.
\item \textbf{An evaluation that includes what did not work}
  (Section~\ref{sec:eval}): synthetic and real name lists, two compilers, and
  an ablation of every design choice against a plain MSD radix sort measured
  in the same interleaved runs.
\item \textbf{Sorts the standard libraries lack}
  (Section~\ref{sec:numeric}): LSD radix sort, a sampled bucket sort and a
  bitonic sort for any $n$ with an AVX2 kernel, for integers and
  floating-point numbers in IEEE~754 total order; and, built on the bitonic
  network, a bitonic merge sort, a bitonic bucket sort and a GPU bitonic sort
  (OpenCL) that needs no SDK at build time.
\item \textbf{Reproducibility}: the library, the tables' generator, the
  harness and the scripts that produce every number, table and figure of
  this paper are public (Section~\ref{sec:avail}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{What standard libraries offer.} Every mainstream standard library
sorts by comparison only (Table~\ref{tab:stdlib}). Radix sort appears in
add-on libraries such as Boost.Sort~\cite{boostsort} and in GPU
libraries~\cite{satish2009gpu,merrill2011radix}. A radix-sort path for
primitive arrays in Java's dual-pivot quicksort has been under review since
2021~\cite{jdk8266431}; we could not confirm that it has been released.

\paragraph{String sorting.} MSD radix sort distributes strings by their
first byte into 256 buckets (plus one for strings that end), then recurses
on each bucket with the next byte~\cite{mcilroy1993radix,knuth1998taocp3}.
It reads each byte that distinguishes a string from its neighbours about
once, but every pass pays for a 257-entry count, which dominates on small
buckets; implementations therefore switch to insertion sort or multikey
quicksort~\cite{bentley1997strings} below a few dozen strings, and
cache-conscious variants keep the counts and the moved data
small~\cite{karkkainen2008radix}. Burstsort~\cite{sinha2004burstsort} builds
a trie of string prefixes whose leaves are buckets that ``burst'' into
subtries when they overflow.

\paragraph{Distribution sorts with a model.} Samplesort~\cite{frazer1970samplesort}
draws a sample, sorts it and uses evenly spaced sample elements as bucket
boundaries; super scalar samplesort~\cite{sanders2004superscalar} and
IPS$^4$o~\cite{axtmann2017ips4o} make the classification branch-free and
in-place. Learned sort~\cite{kristo2020learned} replaces the sample's
splitters by a model of the key distribution trained on the sample. Name sort
is a samplesort whose model is a prefix trie with a prior from outside the
input.

\section{The distribution of names}\label{sec:data}

We use the 2010 US Census list of \so{data@surnames} surnames, each held by
at least 100 people and covering about \so{data@people} million
people~\cite{census2010}, and the 1990 Census lists of \so{data@female}
female and \so{data@male} male first names~\cite{census1990}. Both are in
the public domain.

The first letter alone is very uneven: weighted by the people who carry them,
surnames begin with M (\so{share@M}\,\%), S (\so{share@S}\,\%), B
(\so{share@B}\,\%), C (\so{share@C}\,\%) and H (\so{share@H}\,\%) far more
often than with Q (\so{share@Q}\,\%), U (\so{share@U}\,\%) or X
(\so{share@X}\,\%). Equal-width buckets on the first letter would range from
nearly empty to one tenth of the input. Some prefixes are heavy on their own:
\emph{Ma} (\so{share@Ma}\,\%), \emph{Ha} (\so{share@Ha}\,\%), \emph{Mc}
(\so{share@Mc}\,\%), and among three-letter prefixes \emph{Wil}
(\so{share@Wil}\,\%), \emph{Mar} (\so{share@Mar}\,\%), \emph{Smi}
(\so{share@Smi}\,\%), \emph{Joh} (\so{share@Joh}\,\%) and \emph{Sch}
(\so{share@Sch}\,\%). Splitting the heavy first letters on the second and
third letter is what makes buckets of equal expected size possible.

\paragraph{The tables.} A generator script downloads the lists and computes
four tables over 257 symbols: symbol 0 is the end of a name, symbol $1+b$ is
the byte $b$. Table $P_k$, for $k = 1, 2, 3$, gives the share of each symbol
at position $k$ among the names that reach position $k$ (a name of length
$k-1$ contributes the end symbol); table $P_\ast$ counts the symbols at every
position, with one end symbol per name. The names are weighted by the number
of people who carry them, written in title case as people type them
(``Smith''), and mixed half surnames, half first names, so that lists of
either kind and lists of ``Surname Firstname'' are covered. The tables are
stored in parts per million in a C header (four tables of 257 entries,
4\,KiB); the generator produces the
same bytes on every run.

\section{Name sort}\label{sec:namesort}

It has three phases: plan, distribute, finish (Figure~\ref{fig:plan}).

\subsection{Plan}

\paragraph{Sample.} Name sort takes $s = n/128$ names (at least 256, at most
16{,}384) at even strides and sorts them, with the same MSD radix sort used
in the finishing phase.

\paragraph{Estimates.} Let $p$ be a prefix of $d$ symbols, expected to hold
the share $\mu(p)$ of the input, and let the sample hold $s_p$ names with
prefix $p$, of which $s_{pc}$ continue with symbol $c$. Name sort estimates
\begin{equation}
\mu(pc) = \mu(p)\cdot\frac{s_{pc} + \alpha\,\pi_{d+1}(c)}{s_p + \alpha},
\end{equation}
where $\pi_{d+1}$ is the prior for position $d+1$: $P_{d+1}$ mixed $99:1$
with $P_\ast$, so that no byte has probability zero, and $\alpha = 16$ is the
weight of the prior in sampled names. With many sampled names the sample
dominates; for prefixes the sample barely sees, the Census tables do. For
the ASCII-caseless order the priors of A--Z are folded into a--z.

\paragraph{Trie.} The root block is a lookup table with 257 entries, one per
symbol at position 1. A symbol $c$ whose child is expected to hold more than
$t = 32$ names ($n\,\mu(pc) > t$), and which the sample has seen at least
once, gets a child block for the next position, recursively, down to depth
three. Every other entry points to a leaf, and a light symbol joins the leaf
of its left neighbour while the two together stay below $t$ expected names,
so a block needs only a few leaves. The number of blocks is capped at 1{,}024
(1\,MiB of tables). The leaves, in symbol order, are then packed greedily
into buckets of about $t$ expected names, and each bucket records the number
of leading bytes that all its names share: the common prefix of its first
and last key.

\subsection{Distribute}

One pass walks the trie for every name, at most three table lookups on its
first three bytes, stores the bucket id and counts the names per bucket; a
prefix sum and a second pass scatter the (pointer, length) records into a
buffer. Both passes are linear and read each name's first bytes only.

\subsection{Finish}

Buckets of up to 32 names are sorted by introsort with insertion sort for
small ranges~\cite{musser1997introsort}; larger buckets by MSD radix sort
with an explicit stack, falling back to introsort below 32 names. Both start
comparing after the bucket's shared prefix. A bucket the plan misjudged, such
as one holding every \emph{Smith} of a long list, thus costs time linear in
the bytes that distinguish its names, not quadratic.

Below $n_0 = 131{,}072$ names name sort does not plan at all and runs its MSD
radix sort on the whole input; Section~\ref{sec:ablation} shows why.

\subsection{Order and correctness}

The default order is byte order, \texttt{memcmp} with the shorter name first
on a tie, which for UTF-8 is code point order. With the caseless flag, A--Z
compare as a--z and names equal up to case are ordered by their bytes. Both
are total orders.

\begin{lemma}
If name $a$ precedes name $b$ in the sort order, then $a$'s bucket is not
after $b$'s, and all names in a bucket agree on the bucket's shared prefix.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The trie classifies a name by its first (at most three) symbols, folded in
the caseless order, and its blocks are indexed by symbol, so the leaf order
is the order of those prefixes, with the end symbol first. Packing preserves
leaf order, so the bucket id is a monotone function of the name, which
proves the first claim. A bucket covers a contiguous range of keys between
its first leaf's lowest and its last leaf's highest prefix, so every name in
it starts with the common prefix of those two. Names equal up to case have
the same folded symbols and land in the same bucket, where the byte
tie-break orders them.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Cost}

Planning sorts $s \le n/128$ names and fills at most 1{,}024 blocks of 257
entries; distribution is two linear passes. The finishing cost is that of
the bucket sorts: at most a $32\times32$ insertion sort per small bucket, and
for a large bucket an MSD radix sort, linear in the distinguishing bytes plus
a 257-entry count per pass. Memory beyond the input is one record and one
bucket id per name ($n \times 20$ bytes on 64-bit systems) plus the tables.

\subsection{Unicode and collation}

Because name sort works on bytes, any UTF-8 input sorts correctly in code
point order; a multi-byte character simply takes more trie positions, and
the sample supplies the statistics the ASCII-only Census tables lack.
Language-specific order (Hungarian \emph{cs} after \emph{c}, German \"a with
a) is a collation question~\cite{uts10}: the caller turns each name into a
collation key, a byte string whose byte order is the locale's order, and
name-sorts the keys. The tables then no longer apply and the sample does all
the work; Section~\ref{sec:real} measures that situation on multilingual
input.

\section{Sorts the standard libraries lack}\label{sec:numeric}

All three sort 32- and 64-bit unsigned and signed integers and
floating-point numbers, by sorting unsigned keys: signed integers flip the
sign bit, floating-point numbers flip the sign bit of positive values and all
bits of negative ones. The result is IEEE~754 totalOrder~\cite{ieee754}
($-\mathrm{NaN} < -\infty < \dots < -0 < +0 < \dots < +\infty <
+\mathrm{NaN}$); bits are moved, never changed, so NaN payloads survive.
Every function takes an optional scratch buffer and falls back to an in-place
introsort if an allocation fails.

\paragraph{LSD radix sort.} Eight-bit digits. One read pass encodes the keys
and builds the histograms of all digits at once; a digit on which all keys
agree is skipped, so small values, few distinct values and shared high bytes
cost fewer passes~\cite{knuth1998taocp3}. Keys move between the array and the
buffer once per remaining digit, and the last copy back decodes. Below 256
keys it falls back to introsort.

\paragraph{Bucket sort.} Classic bucket sort cuts $[\min, \max]$ into
equal-width buckets and works for uniform data only. Ours sorts a strided
sample of 1{,}024 keys, takes 64 equal-depth splitters from it, and cuts
every segment between two splitters into equal-width buckets of about 32
expected keys. A branch-free binary search finds the segment, a
multiplication the bucket; the map is monotone, so after a counting pass, a
scatter pass and an introsort per bucket the data is sorted, and a
misjudged bucket only costs a larger introsort.

\paragraph{Bitonic sort for any $n$.} Batcher's bitonic
network~\cite{batcher1968networks} sorts $2^k$ keys with
$O(n \log^2 n)$ compare-exchanges in a fixed pattern, which suits SIMD
units and GPUs~\cite{chhugani2008simd,satish2009gpu}. We use the formulation
in which every comparator puts the smaller key at the lower index: for each
block size $k = 2, 4, \dots$ a \emph{flip} stage compares $i$ with
$i \oplus (k-1)$, and half-cleaner stages compare $i$ with $i + j$ for
$j = k/4, \dots, 1$. Picture the array padded to the next power of two with
$+\infty$: a comparator whose upper index lies in the padding compares
against $+\infty$ and never moves anything, so it can be skipped, and the
network sorts any $n$ exactly, without padding memory. The AVX2 kernel,
selected at run time, compares contiguous ranges of 8 (32-bit) or 4 (64-bit)
keys with vector min/max, reverses the mirrored half of a flip stage in a
register, and runs all distances below one vector width in registers between
one load and one store. AVX2 has no unsigned 64-bit comparison; we compare
with the sign bits flipped.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Machine and compilers.} An AMD Ryzen 5 2600X (Zen+, 6 cores,
32\,KiB L1d and 512\,KiB L2 per core, 2$\times$8\,MiB L3) with 16\,GB of
DDR4 under Windows 10 (build 19045), the machine of our earlier
studies. MinGW-w64 GCC 13.2 and Microsoft Visual C++ 19.41 (Visual
Studio 2022), CMake Release builds (\texttt{-O3} and \texttt{/O2}); the AVX2
kernels alone are compiled with \texttt{-mavx2} or \texttt{/arch:AVX2}. The
GPU is an NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1660 SUPER (6\,GB, driver 576.02) on PCIe~3.0,
used through OpenCL; the library loads the OpenCL runtime when it first needs
it, and the timings include copying the keys to the GPU and back.

\paragraph{Timing.} For each input, every algorithm first runs once
untimed and its result is compared with \texttt{std::sort}'s. Then the timed
runs of all algorithms are interleaved round-robin, so that slow periods of
the machine affect them alike~\cite{mytkowicz2009wrongdata,hoefler2015benchmarking};
each run sorts a fresh copy of the input, and the copy is not timed. We
report the median of 7 runs (9 in the ablation) in nanoseconds per element,
with the interquartile range in the figures. The thread is pinned to one core
and runs at high priority. Separate runs of the same configuration differed
by up to about 15\,\% in exploratory runs, so we compare algorithms only
within one run, and the ablation measures the MSD radix baseline in every
run.

\paragraph{Baselines.} \texttt{std::sort} (introsort), and for names a plain
MSD radix sort on string views: 257 buckets per level and insertion sort
below 32 names, without plan or sample. For numbers also
\texttt{std::stable\_sort} and \texttt{qsort}.

\paragraph{Name lists.} \emph{Synthetic}: ``Surname Firstname'' pairs, each
part drawn independently by its Census frequency, $10^6$ names; the sizes
below $10^6$ are prefixes of the list. Its names are real, its pairs and its
frequencies are realistic, but it is drawn from the data the tables were
built from, which favours the tables. \emph{Census}: the \so{data@surnames}
Census surnames themselves, each once, in five orders: shuffled, popularity
order (as published), sorted, reversed, and in upper case as the Census
writes them. \emph{Multilingual}: $10^6$ file-name-like strings built from 47
words in 15 scripts (Czech, German, Polish, Turkish, Greek, Cyrillic, Chinese,
Japanese, Korean, Arabic, Hebrew, Thai and others), 79\,\% of them with
non-ASCII characters; it tests the byte-level path where the tables know
nothing.

\paragraph{Numeric inputs.} $2^{10}$ to $2^{24}$ keys: uniform, skewed
(log-normal, $\mu = 10$, $\sigma = 2.5$), sorted, and few-unique (16
values). Bitonic sort is measured up to $2^{22}$.

\paragraph{Correctness.} Every timed result matched \texttt{std::sort}
(\so{num@gcc@allok} numeric results with GCC, \so{num@msvc@allok} with MSVC).
Besides, the unit tests compare every sort with \texttt{qsort} bit for bit on
all sizes up to 1{,}025 and on special floating-point values, and name sort in
both orders on adversarial inputs (shared prefixes of 300 bytes, embedded NUL
bytes, empty names, UTF-8); every real list was also sorted in the caseless
order and checked (\so{real@allok} correct).

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} changes one choice at a time.

\paragraph{Depth.} A deeper trie cuts more and more even buckets: at
$10^6$ synthetic names, depth 8 cuts \so{abl@depth8@names-1m@buckets} buckets
whose largest holds \so{abl@depth8@names-1m@largest} names, against
\so{abl@default@names-1m@buckets} and \so{abl@default@names-1m@largest} at
depth 3. But it is slower: \so{abl@depth4@names-1m@ns} (depth 4) and
\so{abl@depth8@names-1m@ns} (depth 8) against \so{abl@default@names-1m@ns}\,ns
per name. Each level is another lookup in a 1\,KiB block, and with thousands
of blocks those lookups miss the cache; an MSD radix sort of a large bucket
is cheaper than the deeper trie that would have avoided it. The Census
surnames show the same (\so{abl@depth8@census-shuffled@ns} against
\so{abl@default@census-shuffled@ns}\,ns); on the multilingual list, whose few
distinct words leave little to split, depth makes little difference. Two levels
cost about the same as three on the synthetic list
(\so{abl@depth2@names-1m@ns}\,ns) and are slightly faster on the short Census
surnames (\so{abl@depth2@census-shuffled@ns}\,ns), where the third level
rarely finds anything heavy enough to split.

\paragraph{The frequency tables.} This is the central question. On the
two lists of English names the answer is that the tables add nothing
measurable. Without them (prior weight 0), name sort takes
\so{abl@sample-only@names-1m@ns} and \so{abl@sample-only@census-shuffled@ns}\,ns
per name on the synthetic and Census lists; with them, the default,
\so{abl@default@names-1m@ns} and \so{abl@default@census-shuffled@ns}; with the
tables alone (prior weight $10^9$), \so{abl@tables-only@names-1m@ns} and
\so{abl@tables-only@census-shuffled@ns}. These are within about 5\,\% of each
other, inside the run-to-run noise: the sample of $n/128$ names finds the
heavy prefixes by itself, even on the synthetic list, which was drawn from the
same Census data as the tables. On the multilingual list the three settings
differ: \so{abl@default@intl-1m@ns}\,ns for the blend,
\so{abl@sample-only@intl-1m@ns} for the sample alone and
\so{abl@tables-only@intl-1m@ns} for the tables alone. The ASCII tables alone
put the boundaries in the wrong places there. That the blend still beats the
sample alone is not knowledge about names. Its any-position table gives every
byte a little probability mass, which smooths the estimates for prefixes the
sample saw only once or twice. A uniform prior would likely do the same. The
name-frequency tables could still matter where no sample is affordable, for
example for input that arrives as a stream.

\paragraph{Finishing and sample size.} Finishing buckets of up to 256
names by introsort instead of MSD radix sort costs
\so{abl@introsort-buckets@names-1m@ns} against \so{abl@default@names-1m@ns}\,ns
per name (synthetic) and \so{abl@introsort-buckets@census-shuffled@ns} against
\so{abl@default@census-shuffled@ns} (Census): on names, even small buckets
are better sorted byte by byte. A larger sample ($n/16$) does not cut better
buckets and costs \so{abl@sample16@names-1m@ns}\,ns per name.

\paragraph{Where planning starts to pay.} With the threshold removed,
name sort plans at every size (dashed in Figure~\ref{fig:names}). Measured
against plain MSD radix sort in the same run, it stays faster from
\so{abl@cross@names-1m} synthetic names on, and from \so{abl@cross@intl-1m}
multilingual names on; on the \so{data@surnames} Census surnames it never
does. Where planning starts to pay thus
depends on the names: on long ``Surname Firstname'' strings from about half a
million, on the multilingual list, whose names share long prefixes, already
from tens of thousands, and on short unique surnames not at all. The library's
fixed threshold of 131{,}072 names came from exploratory runs and fits none of
the three well; a threshold derived from the sample (average name length, how
many names share their first bytes) would be the better design.

\paragraph{Bitonic hybrids and a GPU.} A separate GCC run
(Table~\ref{tab:hybrids}) measures three uses of the bitonic network that the
library added after the main study. \emph{Bitonic merge sort} sorts blocks of
128 keys by the network and merges the runs with an AVX2 merge network that
merges a vector of keys at a time~\cite{chhugani2008simd}, first inside the
L2 cache, then across memory. On u32 keys it is
\so{h031@u32@uniform@16777216@bitonic_merge@x}$\times$ faster than
\texttt{std::sort} at $2^{24}$ keys and \so{h031@u32@uniform@65536@bitonic_merge@x}$\times$
at $2^{16}$; on 64-bit keys, with half the lanes per vector,
\so{h031@u64@uniform@16777216@bitonic_merge@x}$\times$. \emph{Bitonic bucket
sort} finishes the buckets of bucket sort with the network instead of
introsort. No bucket size from 32 to 1{,}024 made it competitive
(\so{h031@u32@uniform@16777216@bitonic_bucket@x}$\times$ on u32 at $2^{24}$):
scattering into thousands of buckets costs more than the networks save.
\emph{Bitonic sort on a GPU}, an NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1660 SUPER through
OpenCL, sorts blocks of 1{,}024 keys in local memory and finishes with global
and local merge stages. How fast it is depends on how often it runs.
Interleaved with the CPU sorts, as in Table~\ref{tab:hybrids}, the GPU idles
for up to a second between its runs and lowers its clocks; runs of a few
milliseconds then finish before the clocks are back up, and at sizes between
about $2^{20}$ and $2^{22}$ keys its median is several times worse than its
best runs. Even so it is \so{h031@u32@uniform@16777216@bitonic_gpu@x}$\times$
faster than \texttt{std::sort} on $2^{24}$ u32 keys. Run back to back, the
GPU's times are steady: \so{warm@u32@16777216}\,ns per key at $2^{24}$ u32 keys
(\so{warm@u32@16777216@x}$\times$ faster than \texttt{std::sort}) and
\so{warm@u64@16777216}\,ns at $2^{24}$ u64 keys
(\so{warm@u64@16777216@x}$\times$), copies included, and it is faster than
every CPU sort in the study from \so{warm@u32@from} u32 keys and
\so{warm@u64@from} u64 keys on. The library sends bitonic sorts to the GPU by
default from 65{,}536 keys on, a margin for a GPU that has to clock up first.

\section{Threats to validity}

All measurements come from one machine; processors with a larger last-level
cache or more memory bandwidth will move the points where planning, radix
sort and bitonic sort start or stop paying. The synthetic names are drawn
from the data the priors were built from, which favours the priors; the
ablation shows that even so they do not matter measurably. The Census list
holds each surname once, while a real register repeats common names, which
favours MSD radix sort less than our Census list does and the plan more.
We had no real list of people from outside the United States; the
multilingual list consists of words, not names. The baselines are one
implementation each; a tuned string sort such as the one of
K\"arkk\"ainen and Rantala~\cite{karkkainen2008radix} would be a stronger
baseline than our plain MSD radix sort, and would likely narrow name sort's
lead on large inputs. The GPU also drives the machine's display, and its times
are the least stable in this study: between repetitions of the same
configuration they can differ several-fold, which we attribute to the
display and to the GPU's power states. We report medians, and the copies to
and from the device are always included; a GPU that only computes, or data
that stays on the device, would be faster. Finally, all CPU times are
single-threaded.

\section{Conclusion}

Person names are unevenly distributed, and a bucket sort that cuts its
buckets from the distribution of their first three letters is fast: about
$3\times$ faster than \texttt{std::sort} on a million names, and faster than
a plain MSD radix sort on long or non-English names. But the evaluation
attributes that speed to the bucket structure and the MSD radix sort that
finishes it, not to knowing the distribution in advance: a small sample of
the input finds the same boundaries as Census frequency tables, deeper and
more even plans are slower, and on short, unique surnames a plain MSD radix
sort is better. A distribution model fitted outside the input, the idea that
motivated name sort, did not pay on this hardware.

The numeric sorts confirm that the standard libraries leave speed on the
table for large arrays of plain keys: LSD radix sort, the AVX2 bitonic merge
sort and, where a GPU is available, a bitonic sort on the GPU are several
times faster than introsort. All of it, including the negative results, is
reproducible from the published library, data and scripts.
